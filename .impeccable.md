## Design Context

### Users

Indie developers, solo founders, and small product teams at startups who want a UI library with a strong design opinion. They're building SaaS products, side projects, and production apps where visual identity matters. They specifically chose a drawn, opinionated aesthetic — this isn't a fallback, it's an intentional rejection of the polished-everything trend.

### Brand Personality

**Honest, functional, crafted.** Built with care, not decoration — beauty here is evidence, not intent: a well-set page looks right because every mark on it is deliberate. No pretense, no filler.

### Aesthetic Direction

**Ink & Instrument.** A printed page you can press. Everything on screen is drawn, not lit: sharp corners, flat color, borders as pen strokes, shadows as offset marks. The reference isn't concrete architecture — it's the print shop and the machine shop: a well-set page, ruled forms, keys that travel when pressed.

**One pen.** The neutral ramp is a single ink. Text, strong borders, and solid fills come from the same pen (tone-11), so lines and fills always agree about what matters. Ink is never diluted, only rationed: a stroke speaks in the ink voice or the hairline voice — there is no middle grey. (Measured, not asserted: at 1px, darkness reads as a switch, not a dial — a "slightly softer black" does not exist at hairline width.) Hue carries meaning but never emphasis; the one vivid voice — the accent — belongs solely to what you can act on.

**The shadow layer is the voice layer.** Elevation is drawn, not lit — and its ink is semantic. Surfaces (cards, overlays) cast a quiet grey shade; pressables are neutral at rest and reveal a thin, sharp **accent edge** on hover. Pressing drops the control onto it. A trigger holding an open menu grows and holds that edge for as long as the menu is out — still pressable, now the active owner of what it opened. Inside that menu, the row you'd act on is marked by an accent rule down its leading edge, leaving the words in ink. Small controls and fused strips are **instruments**: the chassis stays neutral while hover reveals action and the inner key — a check, a thumb, a label — travels 1px under the press. The navigation layer holds the system's glass — quiet type fused on hairlines, with a subtle accent lens over the current place and no accent under the whole strip. A breadcrumb stays plain ink and reserves accent for keyboard focus. Writing fields are quiet until engaged: focusing one is **cap-off**, drawing a static edge that leaves when the pen lifts; the caret carries the vivid voice where the next letter lands. The select trigger stays quiet until its menu is open, while keeping a keyboard ring because it is pressed rather than written in. Focus always owns the outermost mark; open, hover, and selection remain inside it. Softness of the paper, sharpness of the edge, both at once.

**Brutalism is an influence here, not the identity.** We keep its refusals — no gradients, no blur, no rounding, nothing that lies about structure — because print keeps them too. We don't keep its aggression: the goal is not rawness but precision. When a decision feels ambiguous, don't ask "is this brutalist enough?" Ask: is the stroke precise, is the metaphor complete, is there still one pen and one vivid voice?

**Anti-references (what this must NOT look like):**

- shadcn/ui — too soft, too rounded, too similar to what everyone ships
- Material Design — too corporate, too systematic, too Google
- Stripe/Linear — lit, not drawn: glow, gradients, blurred elevation; we share their precision but not their material — nothing on a printed page emits light
- Bootstrap — too generic, no design point of view
- Web-brutalism showcases — aggression as the point; we keep the refusals, not the shout

**Emotional goals:** Users should feel confidence and clarity (I know exactly what's happening), respect for craft (someone cared about this), and material distinction (this looks drawn and set — made, not assembled from the same lit parts as everything else).

### Design Principles

1. **Honesty over decoration** — No gradients, no blur, no rounded corners (except Radio). If it's a border, show the border. If it's a shadow, make it structural (offset, no blur). Ink on paper, structure in plain sight.
2. **Function drives form** — Every visual choice serves usability. Offset shadows are semantic: neutral material says what is pressable; the accent edge says what is being approached or engaged. Buttons reveal it on hover and drop onto it when pressed, open owners hold it grown, writing fields draw it only while engaged (cap-off), and disabled always removes it; the grey shade says "this is a raised surface". Design tokens encode meaning, not aesthetics.
3. **Consistency is kindness** — Same disabled pattern everywhere. One focus grammar — keyboard outline on pressables, cap-off edge on writing fields — and when a field is invalid, danger rides that focus indicator (the ring where a control has one, the edge where it's cap-off) with the border always danger. Same spacing scale. Users learn the system once and trust it completely.
4. **Opinionated defaults, flexible overrides** — Strong defaults that look intentional out of the box, but every component accepts className for customization. The library has a point of view but doesn't trap you.
5. **Craft in the details** — Touch targets that actually work. Keyboard navigation that's complete. ARIA that's correct. The quality is in what you don't see.
